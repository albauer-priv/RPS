## Research Question / Purpose

The review abstract discusses detraining following an insufficient training stimulus lasting longer than 4 weeks, its physiological and performance consequences, and strategies that may help avoid or limit those effects.

## Population / Context

Athletes undergoing an insufficient training stimulus for longer than 4 weeks, including endurance athletes and strength athletes as described in the review abstract; the abstract also refers to highly trained and moderately trained individuals.

## What Was Actually Examined

The review abstract examines physiological and performance changes associated with more than 4 weeks of insufficient training stimulus, including maximal oxygen uptake, cardiovascular and ventilatory characteristics, endurance performance, muscle glycogen and carbohydrate utilisation, lactate threshold, muscle-level adaptations, muscle fibre characteristics, and force production. It also describes reduced-training and cross-training strategies that may limit detraining effects.

## Key Takeaways

- The review abstract describes long-term detraining as a source of both physiological and endurance-performance decline after an insufficient training stimulus lasting longer than 4 weeks.
- The abstract indicates that the extent and speed of adaptation loss differ by adaptation: recently acquired aerobic adaptations may be lost completely, whereas some strength-related capacity may persist longer.
- The abstract-supported preservation strategy is characterized by maintaining training intensity while reducing volume substantially and frequency only moderately; this should be treated as review-level guidance rather than an individualized prescription.

## Important Findings

- The review abstract reports that, after more than 4 weeks of insufficient training stimulus, athletes’ maximal oxygen uptake declines markedly but remains above control values, while recently acquired maximal oxygen uptake gains may be completely lost.
- The abstract reports reductions in blood volume, cardiac dimensions, ventilatory efficiency, stroke volume, and cardiac output, with increased heart rates and impaired endurance performance during long-term detraining.
- The abstract reports that resting muscle glycogen returns to baseline, carbohydrate utilisation increases, and lactate threshold is lowered, although some values remain above those of untrained individuals in highly trained athletes.
- The abstract reports declines in capillarisation, arterial-venous oxygen difference, and oxidative enzyme activities; these adaptations are described as completely reversed in recently trained individuals.
- The abstract reports that force production declines slowly and usually remains above control values for very long periods.
- The abstract states that negative effects can be avoided or limited by reduced-training strategies when training intensity is maintained, frequency is reduced only moderately, and training volume is markedly reduced.
- The abstract reports that cross-training may help maintain training-induced adaptations, with similar-mode exercise suggested for athletes and dissimilar-mode cross-training potentially beneficial for moderately trained individuals.

## Practical Implications for RPS

- For long-duration planning, the abstract supports treating prolonged insufficient training as a potential threat to retained aerobic and endurance-performance adaptations rather than assuming that all adaptations decay at the same rate.
- When discussing reduced-training periods, RPS can cite the abstract’s review-level indication that maintaining intensity, reducing volume substantially, and limiting frequency reduction may help preserve adaptations; this is not a direct individualized coaching prescription.
